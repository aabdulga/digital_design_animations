MOS Transistors as Switches

Every gate on the following pages is built from MOS transistors. For digital logic a transistor is a switch with three terminals: the voltage on the gate G decides whether the drain D and the source S are connected. An NMOS transistor turns on when G = 1; a PMOS transistor turns on when G = 0.

Block view

What it does

The gate does not carry current into the transistor. It only controls the switch: G = 1 closes it, G = 0 opens it. While it is closed, current flows from D to S.

PMOS: the complement

A PMOS transistor is the mirror image: an n-type substrate (an n-well in a real chip) with p+ source and drain regions. Its source sits at VDD, so a gate at 0 is far below the source. That pulls holes (missing electrons, drawn as hollow circles) up under the gate and opens a p-type channel. The bubble on the symbol says: on when the gate is 0.

The highlighted column is the transistor that is on for the current G. With the same gate signal, exactly one of the two conducts: that is the idea behind every CMOS gate.

A voltage-controlled switch

The gate is separated from the silicon by an insulating oxide, so no current flows into it. Its voltage alone opens or closes the path between drain and source. That is why one gate output can drive many transistor gates.

Strong 0, weak 1

An NMOS stops conducting once its source rises to VG − Vt, so it can pull a node fully down to 0 but only up to VDD − Vt. A PMOS is the opposite. CMOS uses PMOS to pull up and NMOS to pull down, so every output is a strong 0 or 1.

Threshold voltage

Vt is the gate voltage at which enough electrons gather under the gate to form a channel. Below it the transistor is off; above it, the higher VG goes, the more electrons and the more current. Logic only uses the two ends: 0 V and VDD.
